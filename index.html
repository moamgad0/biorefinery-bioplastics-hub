Circular Bioeconomy & Lignocellulose Fractionation

Biorefinery & The Circular Bioeconomy: Paradigm Shift from Fossil Resources

To satisfy the energy and material demand of society sustainably requires the implementation of the Bioeconomy, which is based on renewable bio-based resources for the production of materials and energy. Biorefinery is an indispensable technology to implement the blueprint of the Circular Bioeconomy: "A circular bioeconomy is an economy powered by nature" (Biomass → Raw material → Processing → Technologies → Product → Recycle).

⚡ The 3 Main Characteristics of a Biorefinery:

  • 1 Coupled Generation: Simultaneous generation of energy (gaseous or liquid biofuels) and materials (chemicals, food, and feed).
  • 2 Combination of Process Steps: Mechanical processes (such as pressing) and thermochemical processes (such as gasification).
  • 3 Diverse Raw Materials: Use of different raw materials from both virgin and residual sources.

Greenhouse Gases & The Greenhouse Effect

Greenhouse gases (CO₂, H₂O, CH₄): The gases in the atmosphere that raise the earth surface temperature of planets. What distinguishes them from other gases: They absorb the wavelengths of radiation that a planet emits, resulting in the greenhouse effect. A biorefinery closes the carbon loop because photosynthetic plants and microalgae re-absorb emitted CO₂.

🌿 Plant Primary vs. Secondary Metabolites

Primary Metabolites

Role: Involved directly in growth via photosynthesis.

Examples: Lignin and carbohydrates (starch, cellulose, hemicellulose, sugars), Lipids, Proteins, and Nucleic acids.

Form Lignocellulose biomass (most abundant; requires pretreatment).

Secondary Metabolites

Role: Produced for defence purposes; available in small quantities in plants, but of high industrial value.

Examples: Alkaloids, tannins, terpenoids, phenolics, sterols, steroids, essential oils.

Exploited for: Cosmeceuticals, Nutraceuticals, and Pharmaceuticals.

Advantages & Disadvantages of Bioplastics

Advantages vs. Disadvantages (Visual + Underlying Lecture Text)

✅ Advantages

  • Compensate oil limitation & do not consume non-renewable raw materials.
  • Save the environment, reduce non-biodegradable waste & close the environmental cycle.
  • Cover petroleum price increases & provide energy savings in production.
  • Reduce greenhouse gases and lower carbon footprint.
  • Safe in food packaging: Do not contain harmful additives such as phthalates or bisphenol.
  • Fit for all applications, avoid plastic segregation, and useful in marketing.

⚠️ Disadvantages

  • High production cost (Costly).
  • Some types need farmland & risk competing with the food supply.
  • (Partly) use of genetically modified organisms (GMOs).
  • Shorter lifetime & affected by environmental conditions.
  • Lack of right processability: Narrow processing window (lower melting temperature), brittleness, and thermal degradation.
  • Encourage waste increase, need an additional waste stream, and raise compost volume.
Types of Bioplastics

4 Types of Bioplastics (By Source)

1. Cellulose-based Bioplastics

Derived from cellulose acetate, nitrocellulose, and cellulose esters.

2. Starch-based Bioplastics (~50% Market)

Constitutes approximately 50% of the bioplastics market! Starch films are used in packaging consumer goods, bakery items, fruits/vegetables, and magazines.

3. Protein-based Bioplastics

Made from soy, albumin, wheat gluten, etc. Best example: soy protein plastic used in body panels in Ford automobiles.

4. Aliphatic Polyesters Bioplastics

Includes PHA, PHH, PHB, and PHV:
• PLA (Polylactic acid): Derived from sugar like dextrose or maize.
• PHB (Poly 3-HydroxyButyrate): Derived from bacteria processing corn starch, glucose, or wastewater.
• PHA (Polyhydroxyalkanoate): Polyesters produced by fermenting raw vegetable materials with bacterial strains such as Pseudomonas.

Uses of Bioplastics

5 Major Industrial Uses of Bioplastics

1. Packaging

Bags for shopping, waste collection, agricultural foils, nursery products, textiles, toys, and disposable cutleries (cups, plates, salad bowls, food containers).

2. Consumer Durable Industry

Headphones, touch screen computer cases, keyboards, laptops, game consoles, vacuum cleaners, and loudspeakers.

3. Medical Equipment & Dentistry

Robust surgical stitches that dissolve without leaving marks behind; tacks, pins, and screws for healing bones and dermatology surgery; dental implants and tooth filling.

4. Automotive and Aerospace

Transportation components, especially aeroplanes.

5. Cosmetics & Personal Care

Packaging cosmetics products, replacing disposable body/hair care items (toothbrushes, cotton buds, hairbrushes, razors) that end up in landfills.

Genetically Modified Bioplastics

GM Bioplastics & Recombinant DNA Technology

Produced by genetically modifying organisms such as bacteria or yeast to produce specific enzymes or proteins used in the production of biodegradable plastics from renewable resources.

Recombinant Plasmid Steps (A, B, C):
A. Isolation: Plasmid isolated from bacterium + DNA isolated from donor plant/animal cell.
B. Restriction Cleavage: The same specific restriction enzyme fragments both donor DNA and plasmid DNA.
C. Ligation & Cloning: Sticky ends form hydrogen bonds and are sealed into an engineered plasmid (recombinant DNA), introduced into a bacterium that reproduces and clones the gene.
💡 Crucial Note: The use of genetically modified (GM) crops is NOT a technical requirement for the production of any bioplastic materials commercially available today. If used, reasons lie in regional feedstock supply or economic decisions.
